   138→[Heading 2] 1.1 Introduction
   139→[Normal] Technological advancements have long been impacting the manner in which humans think and organise information, as well as creating the products of that thinking. The early tools of thought such as Bush's Memex and Engelbart's Augmentation Framework were examples of tools designed to augment reasoning rather than substitute for it. Cog-docs continue to be examples of tools used for the same purpose — to provide a writing platform that is not simply a collection of characters and words but rather a cognitive space in which to challenge, slow down and augment academic reasoning.
   140→[Normal] With the emergence and increasing acceptance of artificial intelligence (AI) tools, there has also been a significant shift in what people now expect from the writing process. Whereas writing-related technologies previously only assisted users in finding information or checking their grammar/spelling/formatting, today's writing software is able to produce fully developed paragraphs, outlines, explanations and references based on only a few words from its user. Although these types of writing tools have the potential to greatly enhance productivity, they also create challenges to the development of the requisite skills necessary for academic writing. In situations where writing tools do too much of the user's thinking in a very short period of time, there is a strong possibility that users will not practice the skills/concepts required for a quality academic writing product, such as synthesising information, developing arguments, verifying information and revising their work, which are essential components of developing quality academic writing.
   141→[Normal] Cogdoc proposes a model. It is a custom text editor and research-assisted IDE for thought where AI does not write content for the user. Instead it works as a questioning and challenging margin agent. Its role is to interrupt prompts ask questions during moments of writer inactivity and verify written claims against uploaded research material. The project aims to build friction into the academic writing process so that AI support becomes a way of increasing user cognition not bypassing it.

   144→[Heading 2] 1.2 Problem Background
   145→[Normal] Generative AI tools like ChatGPT, Gemini, Grammarly and AI features in document editors are increasingly used by students and researchers for brainstorming, summarizing, rewriting and drafting. Their interface model is usually smooth: the user enters a prompt. Receives fluent output. This interaction appears efficient. The educational cost can be hidden because fluent text can create the impression that understanding has occurred even when the user has not done the intellectual work required to build that understanding.
   146→[Normal] The problem can be explained through offloading. Cognitive offloading is the use of tools or actions to reduce internal cognitive demand. Offloading is not automatically bad. Notes, diagrams, calculators and reference managers are academic tools. However, when offloading replace value cognitive processes like evaluation argument construction and conceptual synthesis the user may become less engaged in the mental activity that academic work is intended to cultivate.
   147→[Normal] Recent research has increased concern about this pattern. A study found that higher confidence in AI is associated with reduced critical thinking effort among knowledge workers. Another study reported that students using language models experienced lower cognitive load but showed weaker reasoning depth compared with students using traditional search. Heavy AI use has also been linked with offloading and reduced critical thinking. These studies do not imply that AI should be banned. They imply that AI writing interfaces must be designed carefully.
   148→[Normal] A second problem is automation bias. When AI-generated text is fluent users may accept its structure, facts and citations without verification. Large language models generate plausible language rather than grounded understanding. In writing this creates a risk of unsupported claims, inaccurate summaries and hallucinated citations. Cogdoc addresses this risk through a Citation Sentinel that compares user-written claims, against research documents.
   149→[Normal] The gap identified in this project is not the absence of AI writing tools. Many already exist. The gap is the absence of a writing interface that deliberately refuses to generate final text and instead uses AI to preserve the user’s cognitive responsibility.
   150→[Heading 2] 1.3 Project Aim

   202→[Heading 2] 2.1 Domain Research
   203→[Heading 3] 2.1.1 Intelligence Amplification and Tools for Thought
   204→[Normal] The beginning of Cogdoc is not the intellectual tradition of AI but of the arduous notion of "intelligence amplification". Bush (1945) envisions the Memex as a ‘personal knowledge system' capable of storing, linking and indexing thoughts by associative trails. It wasn't so much about substituting the thinking process, but on the machine assisting the thinker in dealing with complexity.
   205→[Normal] Engelbart (1962) later encapsulated this in his notion of augmenting human intellect. His writings maintained that computers must "amplify" an individual's capacity to learn about situations that are difficult, and to solve problems. Cogdoc uses this historic claim to the current conversation about generative AI writing. Digital artefacts are not authors, but Cogdoc makes the computer artifact a reflective and adversarial layer around the human author.
   206→[Heading 3] 2.1.2 Cognitive Offloading and Cognitive Atrophy
   207→[Normal] Cognitive offloading refers to using external resources to decrease mental load (Risko & Gilbert, 2016). Offloading can be beneficial in academic settings if it helps relieve low value items of memory, like remembering citation styles or storing research notes. The danger occurs when the high-level task of argumentation, synthesis and evaluation is taken off the shoulders of the person being offered. Allowing AI to determine the structure, evidence and wording for a piece of work might allow a user to write an essay while practicing fewer of the cognitive operations which develop academic competence.
   208→[Normal] Gerlich (2025) offers empirical data which shows that high AI tool usage is associated with cognitive offloading and lower critical thinking patterns. Lee et al. (2025) similarly report that knowledge workers who have higher confidence in generative AI tend to report less critical thinking effort. At the same time, Stadler et al. (2024) demonstrate that the use of LLM can help to decrease cognitive load but can also decrease depth of reasoning in scientific inquiry. Based on these insights, the design decision was made to intentionally slow and challenge Cogdoc down from traditional AI writing assistants.
   209→[Heading 3] 2.1.3 Automation Bias and the Stochastic Parrot Problem
   210→[Normal] Automation bias is the situation in which users believe completely in the automated suggestions even when they are not complete or accurate. This risk is greater with language models, as they tend to produce coherent and grammatically correct text. Bender et al. (2021) state that LLM's produce plausible language sequences without grounded knowledge. This can cause the reader of academic texts to believe persuasive, but unsupported explanations, false claims, or made-up references.
   211→[Normal] Cogdoc directly addresses this issue by using Citation Sentinel. The system does not accept as evidence. Rather, it requires uploaded research documents to serve as evidence for verification. Claims must only be identified as supported if they can be found in a source passage from the user's own research library that is relevant to this claim.
   212→[Heading 3] 2.1.4 Desirable Difficulties and Productive Friction
   213→[Normal] Cogdoc Educational Logic is based on Desirable Difficulties. Bjork (1994) believes that learning conditions that seem harder in the short term improve retention and transfer in the long term. In writing, this is not a failure of the process, it's a part of the process. They are a component of improved thinking skills.
   214→[Normal] Recent research on ‘frictional AI' confirms this. Drawing on the idea of “over-reliance” and the “deskilling” of workers, Cabitza et al. (2024) define frictional AI as a method to prevent dependency and deskilling through interaction protocols that maintain the human's active engagement. Frictional AI is also described as intentional design inefficiency that increases the cognitive involvement in decisions support (Natali, 2024). Cogdoc takes the idea of the AI assistant to the academic writing world by designing AI not as a ghost writer, but as a challenger in the margin instead.
   215→[Heading 3] 2.1.5 Retrieval-Augmented Generation and Evidence-Grounded Writing
   216→[Normal] Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) is appropriate since in academic writing it is important that claims are supported by evidence. For Cogdoc, PDFs are converted to text chunks, embeddings, and stored in a vector database. A user requests that the Citation Sentinel verify a paragraph and the system retrieves the most relevant paragraphs from the library of uploaded content and verifies whether the user's claim is supported, partially supported, or not supported.
   217→[Normal] This does not completely remove hallucination, but it restricts evidence base and makes it verifiable. Cogdoc is designed to reason over a local research library, in contrast to the general training data upon which the model is asked to answer. In academic environments, such as for students and researchers, design is particularly crucial as unsupported claims can undermine the credibility of academic work.

   220→[Heading 3] 2.1.6 Design Principle Derived from the Literature
   221→[Normal] The literature indicates that there is one core design principle for Cogdoc: Academic writing should not be cognitively scripted. The system should help the user by raising poor questions, uncovering weak assumptions, calling for evidence and prompting revision. The final text should still be written by the learner as producing the text is part of the learning process.
   222→[Heading 2] 2.2 Similar Systems / Works
   223→[Normal] To gain an understanding of the current market, three similar systems were reviewed to determine the design gap which Cogdoc is addressing. While not the same competitors, they are all aspects of the AI-powered academic writing landscape: document composition, writing correction, and research discovery.
   224→[Heading 3] 2.2.1 Google Docs with Gemini
   225→[Normal] Google Docs with Gemini offers AI writing tools within the familiar Google Docs document editor. According to Google's documentation, it can write new text, rewrite existing text, summaries, elaborate, rephrase, and format documents based on a text prompt. The most significant advantage is its ease of use; the AI is integrated with the user's writing environment. The design goal is primarily productivity and document generation, though. Unlike Cogdoc, which purposely doesn't create final academic writing for the consumer.

   228→[Heading 3] 2.2.2 Grammarly
   229→[Normal] Grammarly is an AI writing tool for grammar checking, re-writing, tone correction, summarization, drafting and communication enhancement, across numerous applications. It proves helpful to improve clarity and surface level quality. The disadvantage of this, compared to Cogdoc, is its emphasis on improving the quality and speed of writing, but Cogdoc focuses on intentional thinking.    240→[Normal] Cogdoc wants to run across a stack of technologies that enable a custom writing interface, PDF processing, embeddings, vector retrieval, AI-assisted interventions and responsive streaming between client and server. The project is limited by its short development timeline, which is why the chosen stack features fast development, a robust AI ecosystem, and low deployment complexity.

   243→[Heading 3] 2.3.2 Selected Frontend: React with Tiptap
   244→[Normal] The use of the frontend is made more convenient by React with Tiptap being chosen as the rich-text editor library framework, which is headless and is based on ProseMirror. It also has an architecture based on extensions, so that the editor can be customized without taking on the interface of the company. It's appropriate for Cogdoc, as at the heart of the project is the need for a custom writing surface, a Living Margin, custom idle detection, and controls for interaction that are not like the standard AI chat interfaces.
   245→[Heading 3] 2.3.3 Selected Backend: FastAPI
   246→[Normal] FastAPI is chosen as the backend framework as it is a Python-based web framework that is appropriate for the creation of high-performance APIs with automatic documentation and type validation. There's also a vibrant ecosystem around parsing and embedding PDFs, and the integration of AI APIs, such as embedding in RAG pipelines in Python. For this prototype, which is mostly AI-centric, FastAPI is more fitting than Laravel, while Laravel is still a viable option for a standard Web app.
   247→[Heading 3] 2.3.4 Database and Local RAG: Supabase with pgvector
   248→[Normal] For user accounts, document metadata, document text chunks and vector embeddings, Supabase PostgreSQL with the pgvector extension is chosen. According to Supabase documentation, pgvector is an extension for PostgreSQL which enables vector similarity search and retrieval-augmented AI applications. It's a good match for Cogdoc because Citation Sentinel needs to find passages in the source documents that are semantically similar to a user-written statement.
   249→[Heading 3] 2.3.5 AI Services: Gemini API
   250→[Normal] The Gemini ecosystem enables content creation, long-context reasoning, and embedding models, making Gemini API services ideal for AI interventions and embedding workflows. In Cogdoc, the model is not employed for the generation of the final paragraphs. It can be used to categorize the intent of a prompt, make Socratic questions, provide structural scaffolds, and verify claims with evidence. API availability and cost, along with latency, can be used to tweak the exact production model during implementation.
   251→[Heading 3] 2.3.6 Streaming Layer: Server-Sent Events
   252→[Normal] Server-Sent Events (SSE) is recommended for streaming AI interventions from the backend to the front end as the system is primarily used to send one-way updates from the server to the client. This could be an Interceptor response, a Socratic Spacer ghost question, or a Citation Sentinel verification status, etc. If the system has a requirement for bi-directional real-time collaboration, consideration of WebSockets may be considered later.
   253→[Heading 3] 2.3.7 Security and Privacy Considerations
   254→[Normal] Cogdoc processes academic writing and uploaded research PDFs. The system must therefore avoid unnecessary personal data collection, isolate each user's document library, protect uploaded documents, and clearly state that AI outputs are advisory. The prototype should include basic authentication, secure file handling, user-level data separation, and deletion controls. Since the project is academic, no sensitive institutional or confidential research data should be required for testing.

   261→[Normal] This project adopts a Rapid Application Development (RAD) methodology with support of Scrum-lite sprint practices. A strict Waterfall would result in very strong documentation but would be too inflexible for a project where the user feedback, editor interaction design and iterative improvement of the AI behaviors are all crucial.

   265→[Heading 3] 3.1.2 Chosen Methodology: RAD with Scrum-lite
   266→[Normal] RAD is chosen because Cogdoc is an interactive prototype where the value of the writing interface is dependent upon how it feels to use. The system needs to be tested rapidly: design the editor, test the friction behaviour, adjust the prompt rules, and fine-tune the Citation Sentinel. Within RAD, scrum-lite practices will be implemented, such as the weekly sprint goals, a small product backlog, sprint review notes, and incremental builds. This provides the project with sufficient structure while avoiding excessive ceremony for a one-developer academic project.

   290→[Normal] 3.3 Analysis
   291→[Normal] This section gives preliminary analysis of the survey questions collected during the Cogdoc project. The survey aimed to explore academic implications of generative AI, the concept of cognitive offloading and automation bias, the potential for the “illusion of mastery” based on the fact that AI-generated content is often used to draft texts, and the receptivity of its respondents with regards to Cogdoc as a friction-based academic writing tool. Currently, 33 responses are received. The findings of the survey are preliminary because some items in the survey had missing response and the survey is still ongoing. Percentages of items representing demographic and usage variables are based on all respondents and Likert-scale item interpretation is based on valid responses for each of the respective items.
   292→[Normal] 3.3.1 Respondent Demographic Profile
   293→[Normal] The respondent group mainly consisted of undergraduate and postgraduate students, which aligns with the target audience of Cogdoc. Thirty-three respondents (66.7%) responded, 22 of which were undergraduate students. 27.3% of the respondents (9) were Postgraduate students. There were no responses for “Other” and one response did not provide a status. It shows that the goal of the survey is being met, reaching the desired academic user base, particularly students who will need to use generative AI tools in writing, researching and in preparation for assignments.

   297→[Normal] 3.3.2 Generative AI Usage Patterns
   298→[Normal] The consumer survey findings show consumer familiarity with the use of generative AI for its users. 63.6% of respondents (21) indicated that they use generative AI tools daily for academic or research purposes. In comparison to this, a smaller proportion said that they use them weekly, occasionally or rarely. This reinforces the Cogdoc project's relevance by catering to academic users who use generative AI during their writing and research work.
   299→[Normal] The most common academic uses of generative AI were summarizing literature or complex articles, rephrasing sentences or paragraphs, checking grammar and basic editing, brainstorming ideas, drafting sections, generating outlines, and finding preliminary sources or citations.
